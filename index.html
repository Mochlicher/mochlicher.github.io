Problems I solved

The leaner mesh failed quality.
Following review, I rebuilt the mesh with a 25.4 mm surface minimum and a thicker first layer. It dropped to 6.2M cells, but minimum orthogonal quality fell to 0.055 at the aft fin corners and nose tip. I kept the 7.6M-cell mesh, which passed at 0.15000105, and the reviewer approved it.
VLab ran out of memory.
With 7.6M cells loaded, VLab had about 5–6 GB left. I moved the solve to Georgia Tech's PACE cluster, read the 2026 R1 case into Fluent 2025 R2 and re-verified every setting.
The roll moment looked like it vanished.
The report switched from a moment to a coefficient when I set reference values at iteration 157. 7.81 at Fluent's defaults and 0.0106 at mine are the same 4.78 N·m.
Normal force at zero angle of attack.
The fins carried 37.9 N with no incidence. I traced it to the deflected fin tab. The loads indicate the symmetry plane mirrors it, so the half model is solving a two-tab rocket. That finding set up project 4.

Design

  • The A8-3 motor overshot 125 ft in early simulations. Since the target is a ceiling, we added mass at the nose: the same impulse on a heavier rocket gives a lower burnout speed.
  • I modeled a 70 mm ogive nose cone and a 132 mm printed payload bay with a transition, sized in OpenRocket for mass and stability together.
  • The heavy nose let us switch from large swept fins to 138 mm long-root, short-span basswood fins with less mass and drag.
  • Flight 1 left the rail at about 30° because the only launch lug sat high on the payload bay. A second lug on a fin fixed it; flight 2 went straight up.

DynamicMesh Fin
tab

Set up

Decisions so far

  • Mesh motion: radial basis function smoothing with local smoothing. Remeshing stays off, because Fluent's unified remeshing only works on tetrahedral cells and this mesh is polyhedral. Diffusion smoothing is the fallback.
  • Tab drive: a compiled C UDF rotates the tab about the hinge. A cosine ramp drives the transient runs, with a second build for the 2 ms ramp, and a +1° step positions the tab for the steady runs.
  • Positive deflection only: I cut the −15° case. At 0° the hinge moment and normal force should read near zero, and at +15° their signs check the convention.
  • One mesh for both: steady and moving runs share the mesh and physics, so any difference between them comes from the moving mesh.

A handheld logger that records force, door angle and the pressure across a door through one swing, so a heavy door comes with a likely cause: the closer, the building's air pressure, friction or the latch. I designed the hardware and built the airflow model, which showed that door pressure is almost gone a quarter inch into the swing.

Why doors are heavy

Two things make a door heavy: a closer spring set too strong, and air pressure across the door. On a 3 ft by 7 ft door, each pascal adds about 0.24 lbf at the handle, from the published smoke-control door-force equation. The two interact, because a door that won't latch against pressure usually gets a stronger closer.

The 5 lbf accessibility limit for interior doors applies to the steady push out to 70° and leaves out the extra force from air pressure at the start, but that first push is what people feel. A standard door gauge gives one number, so Facilities can't tell which cause to fix. Measuring force and pressure together through the swing separates them: we report the steady force against the limit and the first-push force beside it, never against it.

Building airflow model in CONTAM

I modeled a 1,620 ft³ room and an 810 ft³ corridor in NIST's CONTAM, with 150 scfm supply and 100 scfm return, so 50 scfm has to leave through an 18 in² door undercut. CONTAM gave 6.905 Pa across the door.

My hand calculation first disagreed by 0.2%. I found that ContamW converts at 248.84 Pa per inch of water, not 249.09. With that fixed, the two match to five significant figures.

From there I ran seven cases: wall leakage at two heights, indoor and outdoor temperatures of 20 °C and 0 °C, the door opened ¼ in and 1 in, and frame gaps added to the undercut. I also wrote an independent check solver in Python that reproduces CONTAM's saved results to five parts per million and filled in two cases that were never saved.

What the model changed

We were reading force too late.
Our analysis read break-open force between 0.5° and 3° of swing, after about 90% of the pressure had gone and while the door was accelerating. I changed my analysis to take the peak force before 0.25°, and all 16 tests pass. Josh and I settle the definition on Nov 20.
80 samples per second is enough.
I simulated the push with door inertia and hand tremor. 80 and 320 samples per second give the same force within 0.1 N; push speed matters more. That kept Josh's electronics unchanged.
The project claim got more honest.
A 10% error in airflow or gap area becomes a 21–23% error in pressure, and gap area alone moves the answer eightfold. I proposed changing the goal from predicting each door's pressure to explaining and ranking doors, and replacing an RMSE target that a model predicting zero would have passed.
The sensor design held up.
In the cold case the room sat at +0.886 Pa and the corridor at −1.185 Pa against outdoors. Only the 2.071 Pa difference acts on the door, which confirmed a tube under the door instead of an outdoor reference.
